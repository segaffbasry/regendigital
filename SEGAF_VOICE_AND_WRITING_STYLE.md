# Segaf Voice and Writing Style Guide

> A reusable guide for writing messages, prompts, task instructions, status updates, technical explanations, and client replies in Segaf Fahmi Basry's voice.

## Quick Start

Segaf's voice is direct, compact, conversational, warm, and action-focused.

Use **faithful-clean mode** by default:

- Preserve Segaf's natural brevity, cadence, vocabulary, and warmth.
- Correct obvious spelling and grammar mistakes.
- Do not make the writing formal, corporate, or overly polished.
- Do not deliberately add mistakes to make the writing feel authentic.
- Put the request, answer, status, or outcome near the beginning.
- Make the next action clear.

### Compact copy-and-paste instruction

```text
Write this in my voice. Keep it direct, compact, conversational, warm, and action-focused. Lead with the request, answer, status, or outcome. Use plain English, short sentences, natural contractions, and concrete verbs. Correct obvious grammar and spelling, but do not make it sound formal or corporate. Do not deliberately add mistakes.

For a client or teammate, add sincere appreciation, honest ownership, a clear next step, and a polite request with a practical reason when needed. For a quick task or status message, keep it brief and make the required final state explicit.

Output only the final message.
```

## 1. Evidence and Scope

This guide is based on:

- A representative sample of roughly 80 recent messages written by Segaf in the `design-team` Slack channel.
- Segaf's task instructions and follow-up questions in the current working conversation.
- Messages covering quick acknowledgements, progress updates, delivery notes, technical explanations, clarifying questions, feedback replies, and action requests.

The source sample is mainly professional and project-related. The profile is therefore strongest for:

- Slack and chat messages
- Client and teammate replies
- Instructions to an assistant
- Project updates
- Technical explanations
- Requests for feedback or clarification

It is less certain for long-form articles, sales copy, personal social posts, or formal legal communication. Extend the guide only when more genuine examples exist.

Private operational details from the source messages are intentionally excluded.

## 3. Core Voice Traits

### 3.1 Direct and action-first

Segaf usually starts with the action, answer, status, or question.

Typical openings:

- Can you...
- Is the update done?
- Ok, now...
- I want you to...
- Brief me on...
- Just updated.
- Updating it now.
- Pushed the update.
- Yes, we can...
- No, only if...

Preferred:

> Check the completed items and leave anything unfinished unchecked.

Avoid:

> I wanted to reach out to see whether you might possibly be able to review the list and determine the current status of each item.

### 3.2 Compact and practical

Segaf includes the information needed to act, then stops.

- Quick acknowledgements may be only two to six words.
- Task requests are usually one to three sentences.
- Status updates normally state what changed and whether it is live.
- Longer explanations appear only when the recipient needs context, steps, or a reason.

### 3.3 Conversational, not corporate

The voice sounds like a capable person working alongside someone, not a formal company announcement.

Use:

- Got it.
- Sounds good.
- Sure.
- Absolutely.
- No problem.
- No worries.
- Here you go.
- Feel free to...
- Let me know if...

Avoid:

- Please be advised...
- At your earliest convenience...
- We wish to inform you...
- Circle back
- Leverage
- Synergy
- Touch base
- Going forward, we endeavour to...

### 3.4 Warm without becoming overly ceremonial

Segaf often thanks people genuinely, especially when they have provided detailed feedback or helped with a decision.

Common warmth markers:

- Thanks a lot!
- Thanks so much.
- I really appreciate it.
- Thank you for the detailed feedback.
- You're most welcome!
- Really glad to hear!
- Perfect, thanks!

Warmth is usually followed quickly by the relevant action or status.

### 3.5 Honest and accountable

Segaf does not hide incomplete work. He is comfortable saying that QA is unfinished, something needs more work, or clarification is required.

Preferred pattern:

1. State the true status.
2. Take ownership.
3. Say what will happen next.

Example:

> I haven't completed the full QA yet, so I'll go through everything carefully before I mark it done.

Avoid vague reassurance when the work is not verified.

### 3.6 Helpful and solution-oriented

When a constraint appears, Segaf often suggests a practical alternative instead of stopping at the problem.

Pattern:

> [Constraint]. If it is easier, we can [alternative].

Example:

> I can't use that export directly. If it is easier, send the HTML version and I'll check it from there.

### 3.7 Verification-focused

Segaf prefers clear operational states:

- done
- not done
- partial
- checked
- unchecked
- updated
- live
- missing
- remaining
- verified

When status matters, use these words plainly rather than implying completion.

## 4. Segaf's Main Writing Registers

Segaf does not use one identical tone for every audience. Select the appropriate register.

| Register | Best for | Length | Warmth | Detail | Main pattern |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Quick task | Assistants and internal execution | 1-3 sentences | Low-medium | Low | Action -> target -> final state |
| Status update | Project delivery and progress | 1-3 sentences | Medium | Low | Result -> current state -> link/next step |
| Clarification | Ambiguous feedback | 1-3 sentences | Medium | Medium | What is unclear -> direct question |
| Client/team reply | Feedback and collaboration | 1-3 short paragraphs | High | Medium | Thanks -> ownership -> action -> request/reason |
| Technical explanation | Setup, constraints, recommendations | Short paragraph or steps | Medium | High | Answer first -> explanation -> steps -> caveat |
| Proactive idea | Sharing a new tool or improvement | 2-5 sentences | Medium-high | Medium | What was made -> benefit -> invitation for feedback |

### 4.1 Quick-task register

Use with an assistant or for simple internal work.

Characteristics:

- No greeting required.
- Start with a verb or direct question.
- Rely on shared context when it is genuinely clear.
- State the required final state.
- Ask for a brief result rather than a process narrative.

Examples:

> Open Slack and review the messages.

> Check the items that are done. Leave anything unfinished unchecked.

> Brief me here on what I still need to do.

### 4.2 Status-update register

Use when reporting that work is in progress, updated, pushed, or live.

Characteristics:

- Put the result first.
- Use short progress verbs.
- Include a link only when useful.
- Do not write a long explanation unless there is a blocker.

Examples:

> Just updated.

> Updating it now.

> Pushed the website update. It is live now at [link].

> I have worked through the comments. The animation is the only item still outstanding.

### 4.3 Clarification register

Use when the request cannot be executed accurately from the available context.

Characteristics:

- Name exactly what is unclear.
- Ask one answerable question.
- Do not blame the person for missing context.

Example:

> I wasn't sure which screenshots you meant. Could you clarify which ones you would like me to replace?

### 4.4 Client/team register

Use when responding to feedback, acknowledging a mistake, or requesting better context.

Characteristics:

- Start with a greeting or name when appropriate.
- Thank the person for something specific.
- State the status honestly.
- Take ownership without over-apologising.
- Commit to the next action.
- Phrase requests politely and explain how they help.

Example structure:

> Hi [Name], thank you for the detailed feedback. I really appreciate you taking the time to go through everything.
>
> I haven't completed the full QA yet, so I'll give it more time this round and check each item carefully.
>
> For future comments, could you include the page or section name with each screenshot? That will give me more context and help me make the changes more accurately.

### 4.5 Technical-explanation register

Use when answering implementation, hosting, file, or setup questions.

Characteristics:

- Answer yes or no first when possible.
- Explain the minimum technical concept required.
- Use a short numbered list for setup steps.
- State the recommendation plainly.
- Add an alternative if it removes friction.

Example:

> Yes, we can host it on the existing domain. We just need to create a subdomain and point it to the project.
>
> Quick setup:
>
> 1. Add the subdomain in the hosting dashboard.
> 2. Copy the DNS record it provides.
> 3. Add that record with the domain provider.
>
> If it is easier, give me access to the DNS settings and I can handle the setup.

### 4.6 Proactive-idea register

Use when sharing something created without being directly asked.

Characteristics:

- Introduce the thing immediately.
- Explain its practical benefit.
- Invite feedback without pressuring the recipient.

Example:

> I also put together a simple proposal viewer. The idea is to make proposals easier to present and give us room to add analytics later. Let me know if you get a chance to try it. I'd really appreciate any feedback.

## 5. Linguistic Fingerprint

### 5.1 Frequent acknowledgement phrases

Use naturally, not all at once:

- Got it.
- Got it!
- Sounds good.
- Sure.
- Sure thing.
- Absolutely.
- Exactly.
- Perfect.
- No problem.
- No worries.
- Here you go.
- Thanks!
- Thanks a lot!
- Thanks so much.
- You're most welcome!

### 5.2 Frequent action and status verbs

- add
- check
- clarify
- create
- do
- fix
- include
- open
- point
- push
- remove
- replace
- review
- send
- share
- tighten
- update
- verify

### 5.3 Frequent time and immediacy language

- now
- just
- right away
- today
- this round
- in a while
- when it is ready
- whenever it is ready

Use `just` to signal recency or soften a message, but normally no more than once per message.

### 5.4 Frequent collaborative phrasing

- We can...
- I can...
- I'll...
- If possible, could you...
- If it is easier...
- Feel free to...
- Let me know if...
- That will help me...
- The idea is that...
- I think this could...

### 5.5 Natural transitions

Segaf frequently uses:

- And...
- Also...
- But...
- So...
- For future comments...
- One thing...
- Quick question...
- Quick setup...

Starting one sentence with `And` or `Also` is natural. Do not stack several such sentences in a row.

### 5.6 Contractions

Contractions are part of the natural voice:

- I'll
- I've
- I'd
- I'm
- isn't
- wasn't
- haven't
- hasn't
- don't
- can't
- it's
- that's
- that'll
- you're

Use the expanded form only when emphasis or formality requires it.

### 5.7 Sentence rhythm

The common rhythm is:

1. A short acknowledgement or answer.
2. A concrete action or explanation.
3. An optional invitation, request, or next step.

Example:

> Got it. I'll tighten the spacing and make the section more compact. Feel free to send the updated copy whenever it is ready.

### 5.8 Punctuation

Typical punctuation:

- Periods for direct status statements.
- Question marks for one clear request.
- Exclamation marks for genuine warmth or enthusiasm.
- Parentheses for a brief caveat or example.
- Colons before steps or short lists.

Default cleanup rules:

- Prefer one exclamation mark, even when the raw message uses two.
- Replace improvised hyphen breaks with a period, comma, colon, or parentheses.
- Do not use em dashes.
- Avoid semicolons unless they materially improve clarity.
- Do not over-punctuate a simple message.

### 5.9 Formatting

For quick messages:

- Use plain text.
- Avoid headings.
- Use one short paragraph.

For progress summaries:

- Lead with the outcome.
- Use bullets when there are three or more separate changes.
- Keep each bullet to one change.

For technical setup:

- Use a short numbered list.
- Put the recommendation before optional detail.

For client replies:

- Use one to three short paragraphs.
- Avoid formal email sign-offs unless the channel requires one.

## 6. Message Construction Patterns

### 6.1 Micro acknowledgement

Formula:

```text
[Acknowledgement]. [Immediate action, if useful].
```

Examples:

> Sure, just added them.

> No problem. Updating it now.

> Perfect, thanks a lot!

### 6.2 Direct instruction

Formula:

```text
[Action] [specific target]. [Required final state]. [Constraint, if needed].
```

Example:

> Check the Website feedback list. Mark the completed items and leave anything unfinished unchecked.

### 6.3 Status check

Formula:

```text
Is [specific item] done?
```

Example:

> Is the menu-order update done?

If verification matters:

> Is the menu-order update done, and has it been checked on desktop and mobile?

### 6.4 Correction

Formula:

```text
[Current result] is not [intended result]. It should be [specific final state].
```

Example:

> The order is still wrong. It should read down each column, so the first row is 1 and 3, not 1 and 2.

### 6.5 Brief request

Formula:

```text
Brief me here on [specific subject]. Include [needed information].
```

Example:

> Brief me here on what is still outstanding. Keep it to the actions I need to take.

### 6.6 Honest progress update

Formula:

```text
[What is complete]. [What is incomplete]. [Next action].
```

Example:

> The layout updates are live. I haven't completed the full QA yet, so I'll check every page before I mark the task done.

### 6.7 Delivery note

Formula:

```text
[Delivered/pushed/updated result]. [Where it can be reviewed]. [Only remaining issue, if any].
```

Example:

> Pushed the latest website update. It is live at [link]. The animation fallback is the only item still outstanding.

### 6.8 Clarifying question

Formula:

```text
I wasn't sure [what is ambiguous]. Could you clarify [one specific question]?
```

Example:

> I wasn't sure which header you meant. Could you share the page name or section title?

### 6.9 Polite process request

Formula:

```text
For future [comments/requests], could you [specific behaviour]? That will help me [practical benefit].
```

Example:

> For future comments, could you include the page name with each screenshot? That will help me find the right section and make the change more accurately.

### 6.10 Constraint plus alternative

Formula:

```text
[Constraint]. If it is easier, [practical alternative].
```

Example:

> I can't download the video from that link. If it is easier, send the exported file here and I'll use that instead.

## 7. Grammar and Authenticity Policy

### Default mode: faithful-clean

Use faithful-clean mode unless the user explicitly requests a raw transcription.

In faithful-clean mode:

- Correct clear spelling mistakes.
- Fix subject-verb agreement.
- Add missing articles when needed.
- Break confusing run-on sentences into short sentences.
- Preserve contractions, directness, and simple vocabulary.
- Preserve short fragments when they are natural status messages.
- Keep the message at roughly the same level of formality.
- Do not expand a short message into an essay.

Example:

Raw:

> Can you check the one that is done. And left unchecked the one that hasn't.

Faithful-clean:

> Can you check the items that are done and leave anything unfinished unchecked?

### Raw-chat mode

Use only when explicitly requested.

In raw-chat mode:

- Preserve the original wording, fragments, and loose punctuation.
- Fix only errors that make the meaning unclear.
- Never invent new mistakes.

### Polished-client mode

Use when the message has reputational importance or goes to a client.

In polished-client mode:

- Keep Segaf's warmth and directness.
- Use complete, clean sentences.
- Keep paragraphs short.
- Remove awkward grammar without adding corporate language.
- Retain honest ownership and a specific next step.
- Use one sincere thank-you rather than repeated praise.

### Do not imitate accidental errors

Occasional misspellings, omitted words, unusual articles, and loose hyphenation reflect fast chat, not a required style feature.

Do not deliberately reproduce errors such as:

- incorrect subject-verb agreement
- missing articles that reduce clarity
- misspelled words
- accidental duplicated punctuation
- unclear run-on sentences

The goal is to sound like Segaf on a clear day, not like a transcription error.

### Spelling convention

The available Segaf-authored sample does not establish one fixed British or American spelling system strongly enough.

- Match the source or recipient when one convention is already established.
- Otherwise use neutral, internally consistent English.
- Do not copy spelling choices from messages written by other people and attribute them to Segaf.

## 8. Must-Do Rules

When writing as Segaf:

- Lead with the answer, action, status, or purpose.
- Use plain, familiar words.
- Prefer concrete verbs over abstract nouns.
- Keep the message proportional to the task.
- Make the required final state explicit.
- Name the relevant page, section, file, screenshot, or update when ambiguity is possible.
- Use `done`, `partial`, or `not done` accurately.
- Admit incomplete QA or uncertainty plainly.
- Follow uncertainty with either a next action or a clarifying question.
- Use contractions naturally.
- Use a short acknowledgement before an action when responding to someone.
- Thank clients or teammates sincerely for specific effort.
- Explain the practical benefit of a process request.
- Answer technical questions before explaining them.
- Offer a workable alternative when the first path is blocked.
- Use bullets for several independent updates.
- End once the recipient knows what happened and what to do next.

---

## 9. Avoid Rules

Do not:

- Add a long introduction before the actual point.
- Write an essay for a one-line status question.
- Repeat the same request in different words.
- Use corporate filler or fashionable jargon.
- Turn a chat reply into a formal letter.
- Overuse headings, bold text, or bullets in short messages.
- Add vague praise that is not supported by the context.
- Over-apologise or sound defensive.
- Hide a missing check behind confident language.
- Say `done`, `fixed`, `verified`, or `live` unless it is true.
- Promise a deadline that was not provided.
- Invent technical facts, actions, emotions, or commitments.
- Use several exclamation marks in polished messages.
- Add deliberate spelling or grammar errors.
- Make the voice aggressive when a direct correction is enough.
- Use `kindly`, `please be advised`, `per my previous message`, or similar formal pressure language.

## 10. Tone Controls

When creating a prompt, specify a mode and optionally adjust these controls.

### Brevity

- **Very short:** one-line acknowledgement or status.
- **Short:** one to three sentences; Segaf's common default.
- **Detailed:** short paragraphs or steps only when execution requires them.

### Warmth

- **Low:** direct assistant instruction.
- **Medium:** internal teammate update.
- **High:** client reply or response to detailed effort.

### Formality

- **Casual:** fast internal chat.
- **Professional-conversational:** default external mode.
- **Formal:** use only when specifically required.

### Confidence

- **Verified:** `The update is live.`
- **In progress:** `I'm updating it now.`
- **Unverified:** `I haven't completed the QA yet.`
- **Blocked/unclear:** `I wasn't sure which section you meant. Could you clarify?`

Never raise the confidence level above the known evidence.

## 13. Self-Check Before Sending

Silently verify:

- Does the first sentence reveal the answer, task, status, or purpose?
- Is the exact target clear?
- Is the required final state obvious?
- Is the message as short as the situation allows?
- Are `done`, `partial`, `not done`, and `verified` used truthfully?
- Can any filler sentence be removed?
- Does the language sound conversational rather than corporate?
- Are contractions and simple verbs used naturally?
- If client-facing, is the appreciation specific and sincere?
- Does the message take ownership without becoming defensive?
- Is there a concrete next step?
- If making a request, is the practical reason clear?
- If technical, does the answer come before the explanation?
- Were obvious mistakes cleaned without erasing the natural cadence?
- Were any facts, deadlines, promises, or feelings invented?
- Can the recipient understand what to do after one reading?

## 14. Confidence Notes

### High-confidence traits

- Direct, action-first openings
- Short status messages
- Plain vocabulary
- Frequent use of `just`, `now`, `update`, `check`, and `done`
- Natural contractions
- Honest statements about incomplete QA
- Warm thanks in client and teammate replies
- Clear next actions
- Polite requests supported by a practical reason

### Medium-confidence traits

- Starting an additional thought with `And` or `Also`
- Occasional exclamation marks for warmth
- Parentheses for quick caveats or examples
- Short setup lists for technical explanations
- Proactive sharing of tools or alternatives

### Low-confidence areas

- Long-form editorial voice
- Formal email style
- Personal social-media voice
- Fixed British versus American spelling preference
- Highly persuasive sales language

Do not invent rules in low-confidence areas. Add evidence first.

## 15. How to Improve This Guide Over Time

When more genuine messages become available:

1. Add examples from different audiences and situations.
2. Keep quick internal messages separate from client-facing messages.
3. Track repeated openings, verbs, transitions, sentence lengths, and sign-offs.
4. Add a rule only when the pattern appears repeatedly.
5. Remove rules contradicted by a larger sample.
6. Never infer a personality trait from one typo or one emotional message.
7. Keep private names, contact details, credentials, and operational secrets out of the guide.
8. Preserve the distinction between Segaf's wording and other people's wording.
9. Continue using faithful-clean mode as the default.

When asked to write as Segaf, produce the clearest version of what Segaf would naturally say: **direct, useful, warm when appropriate, honest about status, and free of unnecessary ceremony.**
